**Results paragraph draft**

**How quickly does surprisal-linked curvature coupling happen, and in which signal?** Event-triggered curvature at the standard ±5-word lag resolution (Fig. X, panels A-B, all words) shows both semantic and neural curvature peaking one word after a high-surprisal event (lag+1), but with very different speeds. Semantic curvature (LLaMA-2-7B, layer 18) reacts almost instantaneously and narrowly: a sharp spike at lag+1 (114.6° vs. a 111.5-112° baseline) that is back at baseline by lag+2, with a brief inversion at the event word itself (lag 0) where low-surprisal words show transiently *higher* curvature (113.0°) than high-surprisal words (111.9°). Neural curvature (hippocampal population spiking) reacts more slowly and broadly: high- and low-surprisal trajectories begin separating ~2-3 words before the event, peak at the same lag+1 (98.7° vs. 92.2°), and take until ~lag+3 to reconverge — roughly twice the temporal width of the semantic response, with no inversion. To check whether this effect actually extends further than a ±5-word window could show, we reran the same analysis out to ±15 words. It does not: by about 3 words after the event, the high-surprisal and low-surprisal curves land on top of each other and stay that way all the way out to word 15. Concretely, this means a surprising word leaves no detectable trace in the trajectory beyond the few words immediately following it — words 4 through 15 after a surprising word have the same curvature, on average, as words 4 through 15 after an unsurprising word. So whatever surprisal is doing to the trajectory, it is a brief, self-contained bump rather than something that keeps shaping the trajectory for many words afterward.

**Is this actually a frequency effect in disguise?** Word frequency and surprisal are correlated, so a natural concern is that the entire lag-structure result above is just tracking word frequency rather than surprisal per se. Restricting to a narrow band of word frequency (middle 10% of SUBTLEX population frequency; Fig. X, panels C-D) tests this directly, and despite a roughly 10-fold reduction in event count (1,840+1,841 → 188+188), the answer is no: both signals retain their respective shape — semantic stays narrow/transient, neural stays broad/gradual with no inversion — so the shape difference between the two signals is not an artifact of word frequency. What stands out, though, is that the two signals don't lose the same amount of separation. The peak hi-vs-low-surprisal gap for neural curvature is essentially unchanged by the frequency restriction (6.54° all-words → 5.99° mid-frequency, ~92% retained), whereas the semantic peak gap drops by roughly half to two-thirds (4.42° all-words → 1.2-2.2° mid-frequency, depending on which lag is used). In other words, once word frequency is held fixed, surprisal keeps driving nearly the full-size effect in neural curvature but only a fraction of the effect in semantic curvature — semantic curvature's response to surprisal is more frequency-dependent than neural curvature's is. This mirrors the unique-R² result below, where frequency's word-level contribution dominates surprisal's for semantic curvature (16-98×) but is only comparable to it for neural curvature (0.6-1.5×): the same asymmetry shows up directly in the raw effect size here, not just in the variance-partitioning statistics. (The mid-frequency panels are also noisier at the level of fine lag details — e.g. the nominal peak lag shifts and a small secondary bump appear — but given the 10-fold smaller sample those specific features are not treated as reliable; the separation-magnitude comparison above is.)

**Does frequency's independent contribution depend on the timescale of analysis?** The word-level result above showing the effect survives a frequency restriction is itself timescale-specific, and a broader nested unique-R² test (patient-cluster bootstrap, 2000 resamples) across a 2 (semantic/neural) × 2 (word-level/15-word-chunk-level) × 2 (with/without function-word control) grid shows why: both frequency's and surprisal's unique R² are reliably above zero in every cell (bootstrap CI excludes 0, all p < 0.001) — neither predictor fully subsumes the other at any timescale — but which one dominates flips with chunk size. At the single-word level, frequency's unique contribution exceeds surprisal's by 16-98× for semantic curvature and is roughly on par (0.6-1.5×) for neural curvature. Averaging over 15-word chunks reverses this: surprisal exceeds frequency by 9-22× (semantic) and 1.3-3× (neural), while frequency's unique R² shrinks toward zero under the same averaging. This is consistent with frequency acting as a local, single-word lexical-access effect that chunk-averaging washes out, while surprisal's association with curvature is more evenly distributed across the surrounding words.

**Modulation controls.** Finally, a companion gain-control analysis (Table Y) rules out a simpler alternative explanation for the raw surprisal→neural-curvature correlation (Base, no control: r=0.099, p=1.5e-9, 13/13 patients positive) — that it reflects a trivial gain artifact rather than a change in trajectory direction. The effect survives both additive gain-control (subtracting the mean population vector within each surprisal quintile; r=0.097, p=5.9e-9) and multiplicative/unit-norm gain-control (r=0.100, p=1.3e-10). Notably, a hard middle-decile frequency restriction applied in this same table *does* eliminate the effect (r=0.004, p=0.67, 8/13 patients positive) — on its own this looks like frequency fully explains the surprisal effect, but per the unique-R² analysis above this reflects the loss of ~90% of the data (and the power to detect the small independent surprisal contribution at the word level) rather than evidence that the effect is truly zero once frequency is accounted for.
